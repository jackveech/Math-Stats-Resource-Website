Convergence in Distribution

We need to cover a major concept first before we touch anything else.
Remember in STAT 268 when we discussed expected value? Hopefully.
The idea behind expected value is that if you repeat an experiment n times, where n is large, the random variable would "even out" to be that value, the expected value.
But covergence in distribution is different. This time, we are in the SAME experiment, and the number of times you do the thing you are experimenting on goes to infinity. Or, of course, as before, goes to n, where n is large. So what does this buy us?
Well, sometimes if we do this experiment more and more times, the distribution we see comes out to look like something we know. (In many cases a standard normal, but that's for another day).
We can see that we will approach another distributions curve as we increase n towards infinity. And that is convergence in distribution.
Read: "X1,...,Xn converges in distribution to a standard normal." Drawn as (something) -> N(0,1) with a "d" above the arrow to signify the distribution part.
There is also a "p" version, where something converges in probability to a number, for example, but that's not too relevant in this course, so we will skip it.

A Quick Note on "iid""

We need to quickly cover what "iid" means. You will see it everywhere in this class, but never assume a set of random variables are iid.
A set of random variables are iid if they are "independent and identially distributed." This is rare in reality because nothing useful will be iid.
So for example, the height of men and scores on a test might be iid. The height of men and scores on a test are not at all related (unless the test is your height, but let's assume that's not the case), but both might follow a normal with the same parameters.

In terms of tested content, you should be able to understand what iid actually means when you are told a set of random variables are iid. You can assert in the case some rvs are iid that they all follow, say, N(u, sigma), and then assert some things about those parameters. Otherwise, this isn't that important, and is more of a footnote of the course.

Moment Generating Functions

In STAT 269, you will spend about a week or two on moment generating functions (the MGF). You learned this in 268, but it's worth covering again.

Before we introduce the moment generating function, we must first introduce what a moment is.
Think of moments as a set of measurements that tell you where a "cloud" of data is located, how wide it is, and whether it’s lopsided.
A distribution on its own is USEFUL, but we can only do so much with it. By looking at the moments of a distribution of data, we can gain more insight into what is actually going on.
In fact, E[X], or the expected value, is actually the first moment.

We can derive these ourselves, by taking the first moment as E[X], second as E[X^2], third as E[X^3] and so on, but there is an easier way.
The moment generating function is a function (f(x) = y) that allows you to take the moments of a distribution just by taking the derivative and setting equal to zero.
So if you want to take the second moment, you take the second DERIVATIVE of the mgf, plug in zero, and there is your second moment.
To find the momenet generating function, you need to take the expected value of e^tx. That is, E[e^tx] yields the MGF of a distriubtion with variable x.
Of course, examples of this are seen in the first test walkthrough video.

Covariance and Correlation

Yet another important topic that WILL be tested is covariance and correlation.
Of course we haven't covered covariance yet but I will get correlation out of the way very quickly: correlation is a standarized/unitless version of covariance.
What does that mean? Well, covariance can have units such as l/100km or something silly, which means you need to analyze it in the context of the distribution and its units. This also makes it hard to compare covariance between multiple datasets that might use different units. As such, we use correlation for a unitless version of what you are about to see with covariance.

This explanation assumes you know what variance is. If you don't, refer to the STAT 268 page for a deep dive into moments, expected value, and variance.

Weak Law of Large Numbers

This is the first "new" topic in this class. Formally, the Weak Law of Large Numbers (WLLN) is as follows:

Let X 1 , . . . , X n be i.i.d. random variables with common mean μ and variance ω 2 . For any ε > 0, the limit as n approaches infinity of P (|X n → μ| > ε) is equal to 0.
You have this in your textbook, but I figured I'd remind you.

Note, usually for your first test in this class you will NEED to be familiar with this formal defintion. To stress this: THERE IS a DEFINITION question on the first test. KNOW YOUR DEFINITIONS. PLEASE.
Anyways, what does the WLLN actually mean in real terms? It means the sample mean will appraoch the population mean with infinite (approaching infinite) trials.
You don't need to fret too hard about this chapter, as the applications in this course are relatively minimal (though there are hundreds of applications in statistics as a whole). You should mostly just be familiar with the definition and know what it does.

Central Limit Theorem

The Central Limit Theorem (CLT) is IMPORTANT. PLEASE understant the CLT at all costs. Remember you will probably be asked to define the CLT on your first test, so remember this if you want three free marks (out of 15).
The CLT is formally defined as follows:

Let X1, ..., Xn be iid random variables with E[X] = u and variance (sigma)^2 (where both are finite). Then:

(Sample mean) - (Population mean)/sigma/root n will converge in distribution to a standard normal.

Pretty cool, right? Of course not, because you have no idea what that means. In English, it means as sample size increases, the distribution of sample means approaches a normal (bell-shaped) distribution, regardless of the population’s original distribution shape.
With that said, know the formal definition and know how to apply it. See the first test walkthrough video for an example of how we apply the CLT.

Bivariate Normal, MultiNormal

You actually use these concepts once in the course, but still very important and will appear on your first test.
The multinorm, called bivariate normal if there are only two random variables, is what we call the occurence when two or more random variables together follow a normal. However, there are some caveats. You would expect that two normal random variables would together follow a bivariate normal, but this is not always true. HOWEVER, if two random variables follow a bivariate normal, then for sure the marginals of both the random variables follow a normal.

Note that two random variables following a bivariate normal (in this course we mainly focus on bivariate normal, the same principles apply to trivariate and beyond) the parameters are a mean VECTOR and a COvariance MATRIX. Note that that is NOT the same as a simple mean and variance numeric value.
