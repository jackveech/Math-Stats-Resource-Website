We begin the course with a quick note on what we mean when we say "field." This isn't important now but will be later.
The field is defined as a set containing two operations - addition and multiplication. For this course we can assume the reals (R) and the complex numbers (C) are the only fields you will see.

I'd hope by now you know what a vector is. However, we will cover it again to be sure because some of you are... challenged.

A vector is a quantity that has both magnitide and direction. Of course that's the basic definition, but you can think of it as something in a space pointing from one place to another. It is pointing in a certain direction with a certain overall length.

For example, a vector could be (5,5,5) and it would point from the origin (as is assumed when working with notation) all the way up 5, over 5, and... other over 5 (things get odd when describing 3d so this is what you get). The magnitude is defined in the vector like its direction is. Rather, it's found by taking the NORM of the vector. We will now move on to a very important concept: vector spaces and vector subspaces. The definition and distinction between a vector space and vector subspace is a little (a lot) silly, so let's jump into the definitions:

A vector space is a an entire, self-contained universe of vectors. You can add them together and scale them.
A vector subspace is a "smaller universe" of vectors, basically a smaller chunk of the aforementioned vector space, that must meet certain criteria, including:
- Closure under scaling
- Closure under addition (of vectors), and
- Zero vector contained (read: your space must contain the origin. A vector going from nowhere to nowhere should still go to nowhere in your vector subspace)
